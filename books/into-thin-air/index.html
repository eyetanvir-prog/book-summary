Into Thin Air

A journalist joins a paid Everest expedition and survives a disaster that exposes what happens when a summit becomes a product.

Assignment on the mountain

In the spring of 1996, Krakauer went to Everest for Outside magazine to report on the growing business of guided climbs. He was an experienced climber but had never been that high, which made him both a participant and an outsider. He joined Rob Hall’s Adventure Consultants as a client. Another guided team, Scott Fischer’s Mountain Madness, was on the mountain at the same time, along with several others. On summit day in May, a storm swept the upper slopes while many climbers were still high and descending late. Eight people died over that night and the next day, including Hall and Fischer, both highly experienced guides.

The book is Krakauer’s attempt to understand how that happened, written fast and in obvious pain. He describes the weeks of acclimatization, the strange social life of base camp, the Sherpa staff who did much of the dangerous work, and the clients who had paid large sums for a chance at the top. You learn quickly that the mountain is not a single climb but a long negotiation with the body, which deteriorates above a certain height no matter how fit you are. Judgment, memory, and patience all thin out with the air. Krakauer is candid that he felt his own thinking slow and cloud, which matters for everything that follows.

How a bad day assembles itself

Nothing in the account is one mistake. Fixed ropes were not in place where they were expected. Climbers bunched up at narrow sections and waited. Clients moved slowly. Guides who had set firm turnaround times let people continue past them. Oxygen ran short. Then the weather turned, visibility vanished, and people who would have been safe an hour earlier were caught in the open. Each choice looks defensible in isolation; together they form a trap.

The lesson travels beyond mountaineering. Rules like turnaround times are written in advance precisely because the person on the spot — hypoxic, exhausted, close to a goal they paid for — is the worst-placed person to judge. Commercial pressure adds weight on the wrong side of the scale. A guide whose reputation depends on putting clients on top has reasons to bend. Krakauer does not call anyone a villain, but he is clear that the system made bending easy. He also points to crowding: several teams chose the same summit day, and the queues that resulted ate the margin everyone was relying on.

Disputed memory

The book drew sharp responses. Anatoli Boukreev, a guide on Fischer’s team whom Krakauer criticized for climbing without supplemental oxygen and descending ahead of his clients, co-wrote a rebuttal noting that he later went out alone into the storm and helped bring several climbers back. Many readers came away thinking Boukreev was treated unfairly. Krakauer also admitted that in his original magazine story he had misidentified a climber he thought he saw near camp, an error that mattered deeply to a grieving family.

Those disputes are worth knowing because they show how fragile testimony is in extreme conditions. Everyone on that ridge was oxygen-starved, frozen, and frightened. Krakauer is unusually open about his own failures and guilt, including what he did not do that night. Read his book alongside the fact that others remember differently. That is not a flaw unique to him; it is what survival does to memory. The honest conclusion is that no single account, including the most famous one, is the full record of that night.

The cost of the ticket

Underneath the narrative is a question about access. Should the summit of the world be something you can buy? Krakauer is not neutral — he was himself a paying client — and the book is partly a confession of complicity. It also notes, sometimes too briefly, that Sherpa climbers carry the most risk for the least recognition, a theme later writers and climbers have pushed much further.

You do not need to climb to use this book. Any ambitious project with a deadline, a sunk cost, and a customer has its own version of a turnaround time. Set it while you can think clearly. Honor it when you cannot. And when things go wrong, expect the story to splinter, and be careful about who gets to tell it.

Keep these

  1. 01

    Turnaround times exist because judgment fails at altitude. A rule set at base camp is wiser than a decision made at the top.

  2. 02

    Paying clients change the incentives. A guide whose business depends on summits is under pressure to say yes.

  3. 03

    Small delays stack: a missing rope, a queue, a slow client. Disasters are usually a chain, not a single blunder.

  4. 04

    Experience does not immunize anyone. Some of the most skilled people on the mountain died.

  5. 05

    Survivor accounts conflict. Krakauer’s version was challenged, and he made at least one serious error he later corrected.

  6. 06

    Writing about a tragedy you lived through is testimony and self-examination at once, and both are visible here.
