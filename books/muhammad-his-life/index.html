A biography of the Prophet written in clear, unhurried English prose, built almost entirely from the oldest accounts.

A scholar’s choice of sources

Martin Lings was an English scholar who embraced Islam and took the name Abu Bakr Siraj ad-Din. He spent years working with Arabic manuscripts and wrote on Sufism and Islamic art. When he published this biography in 1983, he made a deliberate choice: build the narrative from the earliest surviving sources — Ibn Ishaq’s biography above all, along with other early chroniclers and the hadith literature — rather than from later retellings.

That choice shapes the whole book. The voice of the old accounts is never far from the surface. Scenes unfold in the order the early biographers set down, often with their small, specific details intact: who was present, what was said, where people stood. Lings’ contribution is selection and arrangement. He turns a large and sometimes repetitive body of early material into a single continuous story an English reader can follow. The craft is mostly invisible, which is a compliment: you feel you are hearing the old storytellers, not watching a modern author at work.

Pacing like a narrative history

The book is divided into many short chapters, each covering an episode or a stretch of time. The effect is momentum. You rarely feel stuck in a single scene, and you rarely lose track of where you are in the larger arc. It begins well before the Prophet’s birth, with the house of Abraham and the history of Mecca, so that the reader understands the sanctuary and the lineage the later story will return to.

Lings writes in a measured English that avoids slang and modern jargon. Some readers find it slightly old-fashioned; most find it fitting. It gives the account a seriousness that does not tip into sermon. He does not pause to defend the faith or answer critics. He narrates, and the narrative is allowed to do its own work. That restraint gives the major moments — the first revelation, the migration to Medina, the return to Mecca — room to stand on their own, without an author leaning over the reader’s shoulder to explain how to feel.

The people around the Prophet

One of the book’s strengths is how vividly it renders the community. Khadijah’s support in the first years, Abu Talib’s protection despite never accepting the message, the loyalty of Abu Bakr, the conversion of Umar, the young Ali in the Prophet’s household — these figures become people rather than names. So do opponents. The leaders of Quraysh are shown with motives a reader can understand, which makes their resistance more comprehensible, not less serious. The household gets the same attention: the Prophet’s wives, his daughters, and the grandchildren he loved appear as part of the story rather than as footnotes to it.

The Medinan chapters keep that texture. The emigrants and the helpers, the tribes of the city, the hypocrites, the delegations arriving from across Arabia — each is given its place. The battles are told clearly but without lingering, and the book gives equal weight to treaties, reconciliations, and domestic life. By the time it reaches the Farewell Pilgrimage and the Prophet’s death, the reader has spent long enough with this community that the ending lands with real weight. Lings tells the final illness and death simply, through the reports of those who were present, and the plainness is what makes those pages so affecting.

Who it serves best

This is a book many people are handed first. It suits a curious reader who knows little and wants a trustworthy, readable account, and it suits a Muslim who grew up with fragments of the story and wants to see the whole of it in order. It is widely read in English-speaking Muslim communities and has been translated into other languages. Teachers like it because it can be assigned whole; readers like it because it reads like a story rather than a textbook.

It is not a work of critical scholarship that weighs every report, nor a legal or theological study. Readers who want detailed source criticism will need other books alongside it. But as a single, continuous telling of the life, rooted in the oldest material and written with care, it does what it sets out to do. Many readers finish it and then start again, which is the best evidence a biography can offer.

  2. 02

    Short chapters move the narrative forward like a well-paced history, which makes the book unusually easy to finish.

  3. 03

    The prose is plain and dignified, closer to the English of a classic translation than to modern academic writing.

  4. 04

    He gives the people around the Prophet — family, companions, opponents — room to be specific individuals.

  5. 05

    The book assumes no background, which makes it a common first biography for curious readers and new Muslims.

    Its restraint is the point: it narrates rather than argues, and trusts the reader to take the measure of the life.
