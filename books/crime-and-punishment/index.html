Crime and Punishment

A theory of the exceptional man, tested on a staircase until the theory cannot stand up.

A student with a permission slip

Raskolnikov is hungry, proud, and the author of an idea he has begun to treat as fate. He believes humanity sorts into ordinary people, who must obey, and extraordinary ones, who may transgress if the future requires it. Napoleon is his example, which is already a tell: he is looking for a costume large enough to cover a pawnbroker’s murder. Dostoevsky gives the idea a fair hearing. The city is cruel. His sister is about to mortgage herself in marriage. The woman he kills is not a charity. The novel’s reply is not a seminar. It is what the idea does to a specific nervous system once there is blood on it.

The crime itself is clumsy, almost amateur, and doubly fatal because the pawnbroker’s sister walks in. A theory of historical greatness cannot metabolize an accident. Raskolnikov wanted a clean transgression and got a second corpse he did not script. From then on he oscillates between the urge to confess and the urge to prove he is the kind of man who would not need to. The oscillation is the punishment. The prison comes later, almost as paperwork.

A city that keeps sending witnesses

Petersburg in this novel is heat, stairwells, and rooms too small for the thoughts people bring into them. Dostoevsky keeps introducing lives that embarrass the theory by being particular. Marmeladov drinks his family into the ground and still tells the truth about his own disgrace. His daughter Sonia sells herself and does not build a philosophy to dignify it. Raskolnikov’s mother and sister arrive with love and with their own traps. Each of them is a person, not a step on the staircase of a great man’s destiny. The more he tries to sort them into the essay, the more feverish he becomes. The body is a critic. It will not let the argument sit.

Porfiry Petrovich, the investigator, understands this better than any accuser with evidence. He has enough to suspect and not enough to close, so he talks. He flatters the essay. He leaves the door open. He bets that a man like Raskolnikov suffers more from not being recognized as exceptional than from the fear of Siberia. It is a dangerous bet, and it is accurate. Raskolnikov nearly loses the contest to Svidrigailov, a cynic who has also stepped over lines and feels no need for a theory. That double is the book’s warning: if you abandon conscience, you do not become Napoleon. You may only become a man who can live with anything, which is a smaller destiny than the essay promised.

The wrong reasons to confess

Sonia reads him the story of Lazarus and asks him to walk into a square and say what he did. The religious frame will not persuade every reader, and it does not need to in order to function in the plot. Sonia is the one person who knows the worst and does not use it to feel sophisticated. She is ruined by other people’s appetites and still refuses the glamour of being beyond good. Raskolnikov wants her the way he wants an audience that will understand his special case. She offers something less flattering: company on the way to telling the truth in public, where the truth will not sound like an article.

His confession, when it comes, is incomplete. He admits the acts more readily than he admits that the theory was a costume for misery and pride. Dostoevsky is honest about that lag. Conversion is not a lightning bolt in the police station. It is a slow failure of the old sentence, continued in a prison camp, beside a woman who came without being asked to worship him. If you want a novel in which the clever man is secretly justified, this one will insult you on purpose. The cleverness is real. It is also how he walked himself into a room with an axe.

What to do with the essay

Anyone who has ever explained a harm in advance — a betrayal called necessary, a cruelty called efficiency, a lie called temporary until the great work is done — has a draft of Raskolnikov’s article in a drawer. The novel’s use is to move the draft into the aftermath. Picture the second person who was not in the theory. Picture the weeks of pretending to your family. Picture the investigator who does not need to win the argument because your sleep has already lost it. Those pictures are a better ethical instrument than a rule you will decorate when you are desperate.

Read the crime slowly, then read the conversations with Sonia and Porfiry as the real action. The plot is a man trying to find a language that lets him remain special. The book removes the languages one by one. What remains is not comfort. It is the possibility of a life that does not require anyone else to be ordinary so that you can be the exception. Dostoevsky will preach on the way there. Let him. The staircase, the pawn ticket, and the fever are the parts that still hold if you decline the sermon.

Keep these

  1. 01

    Raskolnikov’s essay says great men may step over ordinary blood. His fever says his body disagrees.

  2. 02

    The murder is justified in advance and understood only afterward, which is the wrong order for a conscience.

  3. 03

    Poverty is not an excuse he is allowed to monologue past. It is the air the whole book breathes.

  4. 04

    Porfiry does not need a trick. He needs time and a man who wants to be caught by an equal.

  5. 05

    Sonia’s faith is not decoration. It is the only relationship that does not feed the theory.

  6. 06

    Punishment begins before the sentence, in the impossibility of living inside the lie.
