The Diary of a Young Girl

Two years of a teenager’s private writing in hiding, which became one of the most read records of the Holocaust.

A birthday present

Anne Frank received a red-checked autograph book for her thirteenth birthday in June 1942 and began using it as a diary. Her family were German Jews who had moved to Amsterdam after the Nazis came to power. Within weeks of that birthday, her older sister Margot was summoned for a labor camp, and the family went into hiding in the closed-off rear rooms of the building where her father Otto ran his business.

They were soon joined by another family, the van Pels — called the van Daans in the diary — and later by a dentist, Fritz Pfeffer, whom Anne called Dussel. Eight people lived in the Secret Annex for just over two years, kept alive by a small group of Otto’s employees who brought food, news, and books at great risk to themselves. Those helpers, among them Miep Gies and Bep Voskuijl, appear throughout the diary as lifelines to the outside world.

Writing to Kitty

Anne addressed many entries to an imagined friend, Kitty, and the device gives the diary its intimacy. She writes about her quarrels with her mother, her admiration for her father, her irritation with Dussel, the meals of rotting potatoes, the fear of every unexpected noise, and the slow, awkward start of a romance with Peter, the van Pels’ son. The tone shifts entry to entry, from sulky to funny to frightened to startlingly wise. She has a sharp eye for the absurdities of eight people negotiating bathroom schedules, food portions, and silence during working hours.

In 1944 she heard a radio broadcast from the Dutch government in exile urging people to keep their wartime records for after the war. She began revising her diary into a book she hoped to publish, which she planned to call The Secret Annex. She wanted to be a journalist or a writer. Reading the diary with that in mind, you can see her shaping scenes and sharpening sentences. She was already doing the work.

Growing up in a few rooms

What makes the diary more than a document is that you watch someone grow. The early entries are a bright, chatty girl’s. By 1944 she is thinking hard about her own character, about the gap between how others see her and who she feels herself to be, about faith, about the place of women, about whether people are basically good. She judges herself as sharply as she judges anyone else, and she keeps trying to be better.

The confinement amplifies everything. Small rooms turn small disagreements into crises; the lack of privacy makes a moment alone at an attic window precious. She describes the chestnut tree she can see outside, the sky, the bombers overhead. The ordinary teenage struggle to become a person is happening inside a situation of extreme danger, and the diary holds both at once. Readers who first met the book at school often find that rereading it as an adult shifts their sympathies; the mother Anne resents looks different from the other side of the age gap.

After the last entry

The final entry is dated August 1, 1944. Three days later the Annex was raided and its residents arrested. Anne and Margot died at Bergen-Belsen in early 1945, weeks before the camp was liberated. Of the eight, only Otto survived. Miep Gies, one of the helpers, had gathered Anne’s papers after the arrest and kept them, and she gave them to Otto when Anne’s death was confirmed. He published an edited version in 1947. Later editions restored passages he had left out, including some of Anne’s sharper remarks about her mother and her frank reflections on her changing body.

Readers come to the diary knowing the ending, and that knowledge changes every page. Her hopes for after the war, her plans to write, her arguments about the future all sit under a shadow she could not see. That is why the book has endured: it puts a single, specific face on a crime too large to grasp in numbers. You are not reading about six million. You are reading one girl’s sentences, and you understand what was taken.

Keep these

  1. 01

    The diary’s power comes from its ordinariness: a girl writing about school, parents, crushes, and boredom while hiding from genocide.

  2. 02

    Anne was a writer, not just a diarist, and she revised her entries with publication in mind.

  3. 03

    Eight people in a few rooms produce friction, and her honesty about it is part of what makes the book feel true.

  4. 04

    Her inner life grows visibly over two years — sharper, more self-aware, more serious about who she wants to be.

  5. 05

    The silence after the last entry is part of the text. Readers know what she could not.

  6. 06

    It works as history because it never tries to be history. It is one person’s view, recorded in real time.
