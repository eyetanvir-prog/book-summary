The Guns of August

A narrative of the first month of the First World War, when careful plans met reality and every major power found it had guessed wrong.

Open Library note. Published to immediate acclaim in 1962 and the winner of the Pulitzer Prize in 1963, The Guns of August is the classic account of the cataclysmic outbreak of World War I in 1914 and the 30 days of battle that followed. This opening clash determined the future course of the war and shaped the history of our century. Its tense drama continues to enthrall readers of Barbara W. Tuchman's magnificent best-selling work, now in 25th anniversary edition with a new preface by the author.

A funeral and a set of plans

Tuchman opens in 1910 at the funeral of Britain’s King Edward VII, where crowned heads of Europe ride together in a procession of plumes and family ties. It is a picture of an old order about to break. Many of the mourners were related by blood or marriage, and within a few years their countries would be at war with each other. Tuchman uses the scene to show how little family ties counted against national ambition. She then turns to the war plans that each power had prepared for years. Germany’s plan called for a fast sweep through Belgium to defeat France before turning to face Russia. Its logic depended on speed and on Russia mobilizing slowly. France planned an offensive into the lost provinces of Alsace and Lorraine, trusting in spirit and attack.

The book deliberately does not spend long on the assassination in Sarajevo or the Austro-Hungarian and Serbian diplomacy that triggered the crisis. Tuchman is interested in what happened once the machinery started: how mobilization timetables, alliances, and military doctrine turned a regional crisis into a continental war within days, and how each side misjudged what the first weeks would bring. Almost every general staff expected the war to be short. Almost none prepared their governments or publics for the possibility that it would not be.

Belgium says no

The German plan assumed Belgium would let German armies pass or offer only token resistance. Instead the Belgians fought, holding up the advance at the fortresses of Liège and elsewhere, and Britain entered the war partly over Belgian neutrality. German troops responded to resistance with reprisals against civilians, including the burning of the university town of Louvain, which turned opinion abroad sharply against Germany.

Tuchman shows how violence against civilians, intended to frighten, instead hardened resistance and supplied enemies with a cause. It is one of the book’s recurring themes: assumptions about how others will behave, made in comfortable offices before the war, collapsed on contact with real people who refused to play the role they had been assigned. Belgian resistance also bought time for French and British forces to shift, a delay whose importance became clear only weeks later.

Generals and guesses

Tuchman is strongest on character. French commanders kept launching costly offensives while the German right wing swung toward Paris. The British commander was touchy and hesitant, and his small army’s role was shaped as much by politics as by strategy. Communication between allies was poor, and at moments each side seemed to be fighting a different war. The German chief of staff weakened his decisive wing and lost control of distant armies. In the east, Russia invaded East Prussia faster than expected, and was crushed at Tannenberg, partly because orders were sent unencrypted.

There are vivid side episodes, too, like the escape of two German warships through the Mediterranean to Constantinople, which helped draw the Ottoman Empire into the war. The book ends as the German advance stalls before the Marne in September. The quick victory each side expected has failed, and the long war of trenches is about to begin. Tuchman lets the ending land quietly. The point is not who won the month but that the month’s failures decided what the next four years would look like.

History as warning

The Guns of August won a Pulitzer Prize and reached a wide audience. John F. Kennedy is often said to have read it and drawn lessons about miscalculation during the Cuban Missile Crisis. Its lasting point is that leaders can be trapped by their own plans and assumptions, and that a war begun for reasons that seem clear can run far beyond anyone’s intentions.

Read it knowing its limits. Many later historians have also complicated her emphasis on accident and rigid planning, giving more weight to deliberate choices in Berlin, Vienna, and St. Petersburg. Tuchman was a journalist-turned-historian with a gift for narrative, and historians have noted that she relied heavily on memoirs, gave less weight to Austria-Hungary and the eastern origins of the war, and sometimes reached for the telling anecdote over the full picture. Some readers find her portrait of Germany too harsh. As an account of how confident systems fail in a single month, it remains hard to beat. Its lessons about rigid plans, confident assumptions, and the momentum of decisions apply well beyond 1914.

Keep these

  1. 01

    War plans built for a short war locked governments into a long one.

  2. 02

    Timetables can become decisions. Once mobilization started, stopping felt more dangerous than continuing.

  3. 03

    Leaders believed their own assumptions about the enemy and ignored evidence that contradicted them.

  4. 04

    Small events — a missed warship, a delayed order — had outsized consequences.

  5. 05

    Tuchman writes as a storyteller, not an academic, and her focus leaves out much of the war’s origins in the east.

  6. 06

    The book became a warning about miscalculation that later leaders reportedly took to heart.
