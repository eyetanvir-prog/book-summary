The Lean Startup

A method for building new things under uncertainty that treats every plan as a hypothesis and every launch as an experiment.

Open Library note. "Most startups are built to fail. But those failures, according to entrepreneur Eric Ries, are preventable. Startups don't fail because of bad execution, or missed deadlines, or blown budgets. They fail because they are building something nobody wants. Whether they arise from someone's garage or are created within a mature Fortune 500 organization, new ventures, by definition, are designed to create new products or services under conditions of extreme uncertainly.

Planning for what you do not know

Eric Ries starts from a failure he lived through: a company that spent a long time building a polished product nobody wanted. His diagnosis is that traditional management tools assume you know who your customer is and what they need. Forecasts, detailed plans, and milestones work when the problem is execution. A startup’s problem is different. It does not yet know what will work, so the plan is mostly guesswork dressed up in a spreadsheet.

His answer is to treat the business as a set of hypotheses. Two matter most. The value hypothesis asks whether the product actually delivers value to the people who use it. The growth hypothesis asks how new customers will find it. Instead of building everything and then waiting to see, you design the work to test those assumptions as early and cheaply as possible. Ries calls the result validated learning: progress measured by what you now know to be true about customers, not by how much you built.

Build, measure, learn

The core loop is simple to state. Build something, measure how people respond, learn from the result, and feed that into the next build. The trick is to minimize the total time around the loop. That is where the minimum viable product comes in. An MVP is not a stripped-down release; it is the smallest thing that can test a specific assumption. Ries’s examples include a demonstration video used to gauge interest before a product was fully built, and an online shoe store whose founder tested demand by photographing shoes in local shops and buying them only when someone ordered.

Measurement is where most teams fool themselves. Ries distinguishes vanity metrics, such as cumulative signups that only ever go up, from actionable ones that show whether a change caused a change in behavior. He recommends cohort analysis, comparing groups of users who joined at different times, and split tests that isolate one variable. He calls the overall discipline innovation accounting. It is dull on purpose. The point is to make it hard to declare victory just because the graph slopes upward.

Pivot or persevere

Eventually the evidence forces a decision. If the experiments keep showing that a core assumption is wrong, you pivot: change one fundamental element of the strategy, such as the customer segment, the problem you solve, or the way you make money, while keeping what you have learned. If the evidence is encouraging, you persevere. Ries argues that many startups die not from a single bad decision but from avoiding this one, drifting along in a state of mild progress that never becomes either success or a clear failure.

He borrows heavily from lean manufacturing, especially from Toyota. Small batches surface problems sooner than large ones. Continuous deployment lets engineers ship small changes many times a day. The five whys, asking why repeatedly after a failure until you reach a root cause, keeps a team from patching symptoms. The thread through all of it is speed of feedback. Every week spent building without learning is a week of runway spent on a guess.

Where the method bends

The Lean Startup was influential enough that its vocabulary became cliché, and some of the use is lazy. MVP is often used as cover for shipping something half-finished and calling it a test, without ever saying what hypothesis it tests. That is not Ries’s idea; it is the absence of one. The method also fits best where building is cheap and iteration is fast, which mostly means software and services. A drug, a chip, or a bridge cannot be shipped in a rough version every week, though the habit of testing the riskiest assumption first still applies.

Critics also note that some breakthrough products came from founders who ignored early feedback because customers could not yet imagine what they were being offered. That is fair, and it is a reason to use experiments to test behavior rather than to poll opinions. Take the method as a discipline for honesty. Write down what you believe, decide what result would prove you wrong, and run the cheapest test that could produce it. Most plans do not survive that, which is exactly the point.

  1. 01

    A startup is an experiment run under extreme uncertainty, not a smaller version of an established company.

  2. 02

    The goal of early work is learning what customers actually want, and that learning can be measured.

  3. 03

    A minimum viable product is the cheapest thing that tests your riskiest assumption, not a sloppy first release.

  4. 04

    Total signups and page views flatter you. Track the numbers that tell you whether behavior is changing.

  5. 05

    When the evidence keeps disagreeing with the plan, change direction on purpose rather than drifting.

  6. 06

    The method fits software best. Where building is slow and expensive, adapt it rather than copying it.
