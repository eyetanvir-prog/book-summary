To Kill a Mockingbird

A child’s view of a Southern town in the 1930s, and of the trial that shows her what the adults have agreed not to see.

Open Library note. One of the best-loved stories of all time, To Kill a Mockingbird has been translated into more than 40 languages, sold more than 30 million copies worldwide, served as the basis for an enormously popular motion picture, and voted one of the best novels of the 20th century by librarians across the United States.

Summer in Maycomb

Scout Finch is six when the novel begins, living in the slow Alabama town of Maycomb with her brother Jem, their widowed father Atticus, a lawyer, and Calpurnia, the Black housekeeper who raises them as much as anyone does. The first half is mostly childhood: summers with their friend Dill, games and dares, and an obsession with the house down the street where a recluse named Boo Radley supposedly lurks. Small gifts begin turning up in the knothole of a tree.

Harper Lee lets the town seem cozy before she shows you its foundations. The children’s fear of Boo is built from gossip — a monster made of other people’s stories — and they are too young to notice that the adults do the same thing with less excuse. The voice is the adult Scout remembering, so jokes land with a child’s directness and an adult’s irony. That layering is the book’s charm, and also how it smuggles in its argument. By the time the serious plot arrives, you have learned to trust Scout’s eyes, which is exactly what Lee needs.

The case nobody wants

Atticus is appointed to defend Tom Robinson, a Black man accused of raping Mayella Ewell, the daughter of the town’s most despised white family. Everyone understands what the verdict is supposed to be. Atticus takes the case seriously anyway, and the children absorb the consequences: taunts at school, relatives’ disapproval, a mob at the jail one night that is broken up, memorably, by Scout talking to one of its members as a neighbor.

The trial is the novel’s centerpiece, and Lee stages it carefully. Atticus’s questions make the evidence plain: the injuries do not match the account, and the story of what happened in that house points somewhere very different. The children, watching from the balcony among the Black spectators, see the truth laid out and then watch the jury ignore it. Jem in particular cannot absorb that facts can be clear and still not matter. That lesson is the real coming of age. The verdict arrives late at night, and the Black spectators in the balcony stand as Atticus leaves — a gesture of respect Scout only half understands.

Courage without a win

Atticus defines courage early, through an unlikely example: a sharp-tongued, ailing old neighbor, Mrs. Dubose, who fights a morphine addiction before she dies. Real courage, he tells Jem, is starting something you know you will probably lose and seeing it through. The trial is that principle at scale. He does not expect to win. He expects to make the town look at what it is doing, and to make the jury take longer than it wanted to.

Lee is careful not to make Maycomb a town of cartoon bigots. Most of the people who accept the verdict are ones the children like. That is the uncomfortable point. Injustice here is not a few bad actors but an arrangement that ordinary, friendly people maintain because it costs them nothing. The mockingbird of the title — a creature that does no harm and only sings — becomes a figure for the innocents that arrangement destroys. What happens to Tom after the trial makes the cost concrete, and the town’s shrug at the news is as damning as the verdict itself.

Standing on the porch

The book’s final movement returns to Boo Radley, and the two plots meet in a way that is better experienced than summarized. Its last scene is a small physical act of empathy: Scout stands where someone else has stood and sees her street as they saw it. Atticus’s advice, that you never really understand a person until you consider things from their point of view, stops being a maxim and becomes a view from a porch.

Readers today rightly push on the book’s limits. Tom Robinson is more symbol than person, the Black characters are seen almost entirely through white eyes, and the story centers a white family’s moral education. Lee’s later-published Go Set a Watchman complicated Atticus further. None of that cancels the novel’s achievement, but it is a better book when read critically, which is, in fact, what it asks of its readers.

Keep these

  1. 01

    The narrator is a child looking back as an adult. The double vision is the source of both the humor and the ache.

  2. 02

    Courage, in Atticus Finch’s sense, is doing the right thing knowing you will probably lose.

  3. 03

    The town is not full of villains. It is full of decent people who accept an indecent arrangement.

  4. 04

    Boo Radley and Tom Robinson are mirror figures: both judged by rumor, both harmed by the community’s certainty.

  5. 05

    Empathy is practiced, not felt. You have to imagine your way into someone’s situation on purpose.

  6. 06

    Read it also for what it leaves out: Tom’s inner life, the Black community’s view, the limits of one good man.
