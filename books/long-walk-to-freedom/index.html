Long Walk to Freedom

Mandela’s account of a life spent fighting apartheid, much of it behind bars, and the patience that outlasted it.

Open Library note. The riveting memoirs of the outstanding moral and political leader of our time, Long Walk to Freedom brilliantly recreates the drama of the experiences that helped shape Nelson Mandela's destiny. Emotive, compelling and uplifting, Long Walk to Freedom is the exhilarating story of an epic life; a story of hardship, resilience and ultimate triumph told with the clarity and eloquence of a born leader.

From Qunu to Johannesburg

Nelson Mandela grew up in the rural Eastern Cape, in the village of Qunu, and was raised after his father’s death in the household of the regent of the Thembu people. He watched the regent hold council, listening to everyone before speaking, and later described that as his first model of leadership. He was educated at mission schools and at Fort Hare, then fled to Johannesburg to avoid an arranged marriage.

In the city he met the realities of segregation head-on. He studied law, worked as a clerk, and with Oliver Tambo opened one of the first Black-run law firms in South Africa. He joined the African National Congress and helped found its Youth League, pushing the organization toward mass action. The early chapters show a young man becoming political through daily experience. He is candid about his younger self: ambitious, somewhat vain about his appearance, and slow at first to see how completely politics would take over his life.

From defiance to armed struggle

After the National Party came to power in 1948 and began building apartheid into law, Mandela helped lead nonviolent campaigns, including the Defiance Campaign of the early 1950s. He was arrested, banned, and tried for treason in a case that lasted years before ending in acquittal. The book is careful to show how the ANC’s choices were debated and how slowly the movement reached its conclusions. The Treason Trial also bound together activists of different races, a coalition that later shaped his vision of a non-racial South Africa.

After police killed protesters at Sharpeville in 1960 and the ANC was banned, Mandela argued that nonviolence alone could no longer work, and he helped found an armed wing, Umkhonto we Sizwe. He went underground, was captured, and in the Rivonia Trial was sentenced to life imprisonment in 1964. His statement from the dock, explaining why he was prepared to die for a democratic, non-racial society, is one of the book’s central moments.

Twenty-seven years

Most of his imprisonment was spent on Robben Island, breaking rocks in a lime quarry, living in a small cell, and allowed few letters and visits. The book treats prison as a continuation of the struggle. Prisoners organized, studied, debated, and pressed for better treatment one concession at a time. Mandela learned Afrikaans and studied the history of his jailers so he could understand and speak to them. He treats the small victories — long trousers instead of shorts, permission to study, access to newspapers — as seriously as the large ones, because in prison the small ones were the terrain.

He is frank about the personal cost. He missed his children’s growing up, could not attend the funerals of his mother and eldest son, and watched his wife Winnie harassed and banished. Later he was moved to Pollsmoor and then Victor Verster prison, where he began secret talks with the government, at first without the knowledge of his colleagues. He explains the risk and why he took it.

Negotiation and freedom

Mandela was released in February 1990. The final chapters cover the tense negotiations that followed, the violence that threatened to derail them, and the first democratic election in 1994, which made him president. He describes working with former enemies not as forgiveness for its own sake but as the only path to a stable country. He is honest, too, about disagreements inside his own movement and about the strain on his marriage, which ended in separation after his release.

He began writing the memoir secretly on Robben Island, and the finished book carries the patience of that origin. It is long, detailed, and deliberate. Its closing reflection is that the walk does not end with release. Being free means respecting and enhancing the freedom of others, and every summit reached reveals more hills to climb. That modesty about victory is perhaps the book’s most useful lesson. It is a reminder that the long work of building a just society begins, rather than ends, when the prison gates open.

Keep these

  1. 01

    The book begins in a rural village and a royal household, and that upbringing shapes his idea of leadership.

  2. 02

    Mandela’s path to armed struggle was gradual and argued, not impulsive.

  3. 03

    Prison was a long campaign of its own: dignity, study, and small negotiations with guards and officials.

  4. 04

    He insists on his flaws — as a husband, as a father — and on the costs his choices imposed on his family.

  5. 05

    Negotiation with former enemies was a strategic choice, not a sentimental one.

  6. 06

    Freedom, in his telling, is not a finish line but a responsibility to secure it for others.
