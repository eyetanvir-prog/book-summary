Into the Wild

A young man gives away his savings, walks into the Alaskan bush, and dies — and a journalist tries to understand him without turning him into a saint or a fool.

Open Library note. In April 1992 a young man from a well-to-do family hitchhiked to Alaska and walked alone into the wilderness north of Mt. McKinley. His name was Christopher Johnson McCandless. He had given $25,000 in savings to charity, abandoned his car and most of his possessions, burned all the cash in his wallet, and invented a new life for himself. Four months later, his decomposed body was found by a moose hunter. How McCandless came to die is the unforgettable story of Into the Wild.

A disappearance with a paper trail

Chris McCandless graduated from Emory University in 1990, donated most of his savings to charity, and vanished from his family’s life. For two years he drifted across the American West under the name Alexander Supertramp — working on a grain operation in South Dakota, paddling toward Mexico, sleeping rough, writing in journals and the margins of Tolstoy, Thoreau, and Jack London. In April 1992 he walked into the bush north of Denali along the Stampede Trail and set up in an abandoned bus. His body was found there that September.

Krakauer first wrote about him for Outside, and the book expands that piece by tracing the route backward. He interviews the people McCandless met: a farmer who employed him, an itinerant couple who treated him like a son, an elderly man who wanted to adopt him. These encounters are the book’s warmest material. They show a charming, stubborn young man who made friends easily and left them abruptly, as if attachment itself were a trap he had to outrun. Several of them wrote to him or tried to keep in touch, and their grief when they learned of his death is some of the plainest, most affecting material in the book.

Reading the reasons

Why go? Krakauer offers several layers. There is the literary romance of self-reliance and the purity of hardship. There is a family history McCandless had discovered and resented, and a deep anger at his father. There is also something temperamental: a taste for risk and a need to prove himself against something that does not negotiate. The book does not settle on one cause, which is honest. People rarely leave for a single reason.

Krakauer’s method includes himself. He spends chapters on his own reckless solo climb in Alaska as a young man and on other wanderers who vanished into wild country. This is the book’s gamble. It helps you see McCandless as part of a recognizable type rather than a freak. It also tilts the scales: Krakauer survived his own risks and is inclined to read McCandless generously. Notice that tilt and you can still learn from the comparison. The comparison also raises an uncomfortable truth: the line between a celebrated adventure and a cautionary tale is often luck.

Planning and its absence

Many Alaskans read the story very differently. To them McCandless was underequipped and overconfident: no detailed map, little food, an ill-suited rifle, and no plan for the river that swelled with summer melt and blocked his way back out. A map would have shown other ways across not far away. The bush does not reward good intentions, and romanticizing his death, critics argue, has drawn others to the same bus with fatal or near-fatal results. Authorities eventually removed it.

The exact cause of death has been disputed for years. Starvation is clear; Krakauer has argued that a toxic compound in wild plants he ate may have weakened him, and his specific explanation changed across editions as he pursued the question. Scientists have disagreed. The fairest reading is that the facts are partly unknown, and that the debate itself reflects how much people want this death to mean something particular. What is not in doubt is that a few practical choices — more food, a map, a way to call for help — would very likely have changed the outcome.

What to take from it

The book is best read neither as a manifesto nor as a cautionary tale but as a portrait of a mind in motion. McCandless wanted a life stripped to essentials and was willing to pay for it, though he did not grasp how high the price could be. His late journal notes hint that his ideas about solitude were shifting toward the value of shared experience. Whether that reading is right or wishful, it is the most moving thread in the book.

If you feel the pull of a dramatic exit, keep two things. The urge to test yourself can be real and worth honoring. And preparation is not a betrayal of that urge; it is how you get to keep having it. The people who loved him, and the ones he met on the road, would have preferred a less pure story with a living ending.
