The Immortal Life of Henrietta Lacks

The story of the woman behind HeLa cells, and of the family who learned decades later what had been taken.

Rebecca SklootFirst published 2009About 381 pagesCard · 1 min

Three ideas to keep

  1. 01

    Skloot shows how cells taken from a poor Black patient without consent became one of medicine’s most important research tools.

  2. 02

    The book puts consent, race, and profit side by side: the cells built careers and industries while the Lacks family struggled to afford healthcare.

  3. 03

    Much of it follows Skloot’s relationship with Henrietta’s daughter Deborah, making the reporting itself part of the ethical question.

Open Library note. Her name was Henrietta Lacks, but scientists know her as HeLa. She was a poor black tobacco farmer whose cells—taken without her knowledge in 1951—became one of the most important tools in medicine, vital for developing the polio vaccine, cloning, gene mapping, in vitro fertilization, and more. Henrietta’s cells have been bought and sold by the billions, yet she remains virtually unknown, and her family can’t afford health insurance.
