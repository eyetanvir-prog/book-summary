The Myth of Sisyphus

An essay that looks straight at a universe without built-in meaning and refuses both despair and the easy exits.

Open Library note. «Il n'y a qu'un problème philosophique vraiment sérieux : c'est le suicide.» Avec cette formule foudroyante, qui semble rayer d'un trait toute la philosophie, un jeune homme de moins de trente ans commence son analyse de la sensibilité absurde. Il décrit le «mal de l'esprit» dont souffre l'époque actuelle : «L'absurde naît de la confrontation de l'appel humain avec le silence déraisonnable du monde.»

The one question first

Camus opens with the starkest question he can find: given what we know about life, is it worth living? He wrote the essay in the early 1940s, with France under occupation, and he treats the question not as a mood but as the first problem of philosophy. Everything else, he says, comes after. That bluntness is the essay’s strength. It does not build up to the hard part. It starts there and asks you to stay.

His name for the situation is the absurd. It arises when a creature that wants clarity, unity, and reasons meets a world that offers none of them. The world is not absurd on its own, and neither are you. The absurd is the relationship, the gap between the asking and the silence. It often shows up in ordinary moments: the commute, the routine, the sudden sense that you are going through the motions and cannot say why. Camus treats that moment as the beginning of honesty, not as a symptom.

Refusing the exits

Once you see the gap, Camus argues, there are tempting ways to close it. One is physical suicide, which ends the question by ending the questioner. Another he calls philosophical suicide: the leap into a belief, religious or otherwise, that promises the meaning the world withholds. He takes thinkers such as Kierkegaard and Jaspers seriously and then accuses them of starting from the absurd and jumping past it, turning the very lack of answers into a reason for faith.

His alternative is to keep the tension alive. Do not deny the longing for meaning, and do not pretend the world has answered it. Live with both. He calls this stance revolt, and he pairs it with freedom and passion. Freedom, because without a script handed down from above, you are not living toward an eternal grade. Passion, because if this life is what there is, the point is to live as much of it, as attentively, as you can.

Figures of the absurd life

Camus sketches a few portraits of people who live this way without illusions. The seducer who does not pretend each affair is eternal, the actor who lives many lives in a short span, the conqueror who acts while knowing action changes nothing final. These are not role models in any moral sense, and the essay is not advice to be a cad. They are illustrations of lucidity: people who know the stakes are finite and live intensely anyway. Camus is more interested in quantity and vividness of experience than in any ranking of noble and base lives, which is one of the places where readers fairly push back.

He also writes about art. The creator, for Camus, is perhaps the most absurd figure of all, building works that explain nothing and will not last forever, and doing it with full commitment. A novel or a painting does not solve the absurd. It describes it, gives it form, and lets someone else recognize it. That is a modest claim for art, and also a defense of it against anyone who says it is useless because it changes nothing ultimate.

One must imagine him

The essay ends with the myth that gives it its title. Sisyphus, punished by the gods, rolls a boulder up a hill, watches it roll back down, and starts again forever. It is the image of futile labor, and Camus chooses it deliberately. He fixes on the walk back down the hill, the pause when Sisyphus is fully aware of his condition. That awareness, he argues, is what makes the fate his own. The gods meant it as torment. Sisyphus answers by refusing to be crushed by it.

Camus concludes that we have to imagine Sisyphus happy. It is a startling line, and it is easy to hear as a slogan. Read in context, it is a claim about where satisfaction lives: in the struggle itself, the rock, the hill, the particular effort of the day, rather than in some final payoff that is not coming. You do not need to share his metaphysics to use that. Many days are a boulder. The question is whether you push it with contempt for your own life or with attention to it.

Keep these

  1. 01

    The absurd is not the world or the mind alone. It is the collision between our demand for meaning and the world’s silence.

  2. 02

    Camus asks whether life is worth living before asking anything else, and he takes the question seriously.

  3. 03

    Leaping to a faith or system that dissolves the problem is, for him, a way of dodging it.

  4. 04

    His answer is revolt: keep the question open, keep living, and refuse to be consoled by a lie.

  5. 05

    A life without ultimate meaning can still be full of intensity, freedom, and attention.

  6. 06

    Happiness, in the final image, is found inside the repeated task, not after it.
